# Writing Descriptions & Titles

The title and description are often the _only_ things a visitor reads before deciding whether to click a resource. They're small fields with an outsized job: on a site used by people in acute grief, a few careless words can send someone to the wrong place or add to their exhaustion. This page is about writing them well.

## Who you're writing for

Picture the reader honestly. They are often grieving, tired, overwhelmed, and scanning — not reading. They have little patience for hype, jargon, or anything that makes them work to understand what a resource is. Your writing should reduce their effort, not add to it.

The voice we aim for is **warm, plain, and honest**. Calm rather than cheerful. Clear rather than clinical. Never salesy, never preachy.

## Titles

**Keep them short and descriptive.** The field warns above 80 characters, but the best titles are far shorter. A title should tell the visitor _what the thing is_ at a glance. However, as we prefer the resource's actual title where practical, this may often be outside of your control.

- Prefer the resource's real name where it has one.
- Cut unnecessary words ("A Helpful Guide to…" → just name the guide).
- Don't pack keywords into the title for search — that's what **Search Aliases** are for (see **Adding & Editing a Resource**).

| Instead of                                                             | Write                                 |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| "An Article That Discusses Coping Strategies for the Loss of a Spouse" | "Coping After the Death of a Spouse"  |
| "AMAZING free grief support group!!"                                   | "Online Grief Support Group (Weekly)" |

## Descriptions

Aim for **around 30 words** — roughly one or two clear sentences. The field warns above 200 characters because long descriptions lose impact and rarely get read. Descriptions are **plain text**: no links, no formatting (links belong in the URL and other fields).

A good description answers, quickly:

- **What is it?**
- **Who's it for?**
- **What will the reader get from it?**

Write it as a neutral, honest summary — not a sales pitch, and not a review.

**Do**

- Lead with the substance: "A short guide to talking to children about a death in the family."
- Be concrete and specific.
- Be honest about scope — if it's aimed at supporters rather than the bereaved, or requires sign-up, say or reflect that.

**Don't**

- Hype it ("the best resource you'll ever find").
- Use clinical or academic language where plain words work.
- Be glib or breezy about grief ("everything happens for a reason").
- Repeat the title verbatim — add information the title doesn't carry.

### A worked example

> **Title:** Grief and the Holidays
>
> **Weak description:** "This is a really wonderful and comprehensive article all about how to deal with the difficult and challenging experience of grief during the holiday season and beyond."
>
> **Better description:** "Practical suggestions for getting through holidays and anniversaries after a loss, including how to handle traditions and other people's expectations."

The second is shorter, tells the reader what they'll actually get, and respects their time.

## Tone: a short checklist

Before you publish, re-read the title and description **as if you were the grieving person searching**:

- [ ] Would this feel _kind_ to someone in pain?
- [ ] Is it _honest_ — no overselling, no false comfort?
- [ ] Is it _clear_ in a single quick read?
- [ ] Is it _plain_ — free of jargon and clichés about grief?
- [ ] Is it _short_ enough to respect a tired reader's attention?
