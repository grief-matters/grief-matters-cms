# The Role of a Content Editor

## What a Content Editor does

As a Content Editor on Why Grief Matters, your job is to build and maintain the curated directory of resources that sits at the heart of the site. In practice that means:

- **Finding** high-quality grief resources on the open internet — articles, books, support groups, crisis services, podcasts, apps, and more.
- **Assessing** them: Is this trustworthy? Is it kind? Is it accurate? Who is it really for, and at what point in their grief?
- **Describing** them honestly and compassionately, in a few clear sentences.
- **Classifying** them with the project's taxonomy so that the people who need each resource can actually find it.
- **Curating** the whole collection over time — keeping it current, removing what no longer serves, and featuring what's exceptional.

The through-line is _judgement_. Most of what you do involves deciding: whether a resource belongs, what it's really about, and who it will help.

## Digital literacy

**The tool is the easy part.** Sanity Studio is deliberately simple: it's a set of forms with clear fields, sensible validation, and guidance written right next to each input. If you're comfortable with everyday digital tasks — filling in online forms, writing an email, doing a bit of web research — you already have the technical skills this role needs. The rest of these docs will fill in the project-specific details, and they'll become second nature within a few sessions.

## Grief Expertise

**The domain is the hard part.** The genuinely demanding, genuinely valuable skill here is an understanding in the domain of grief. Deciding whether a resource is _actually good_. That is why grief expertise is a **prerequisite** for this work, not something we can provide as part of onboarding.

> Put simply: we can teach the Studio to anyone in an afternoon. We cannot teach grief expertise in the same amount of time, and the quality of the whole site depends on it.

## Why editorial judgement matters so much

Everyone who arrives at this site is, in some way, having one of the worst times of their life — or trying to help someone who is. They are often exhausted, overwhelmed, and short on patience for anything that wastes their time or, worse, hurts them.

That raises the stakes of ordinary editorial decisions:

- A resource that is merely _mediocre_ isn't neutral here — it costs a grieving person energy they don't have.
- A resource with the wrong _tone_ — glib, preachy, commercially pushy, or clinical where it should be warm — can do real harm.
- A resource filed under the wrong _classification_ won't reach the person it was meant for, and may surface for someone it could distress.

So the care you bring to selection, description, and classification is the substance of our service.

## When to defer to the project lead

You have wide latitude over individual resources but some decisions should not be made alone:

- **The shape and meaning of the taxonomy** — what each tag is _for_, where one tag ends and another begins — is owned by the project's editorial lead. When you hit a genuine boundary question ("is this _complicated grief_ or _disenfranchised grief_?"), the right move is to raise it, not to quietly pick one and move on. See **Tagging & Classification**.
- **The content model itself** — the fields, types, and structure — is owned by the developers. If a resource doesn't seem to fit any type, or a field seems wrong, flag it rather than forcing it.

## Working alongside the AI Content Editor

Part of the review workload is shared with an automated **AI Content Editor** that checks resources, drafts corrections, and suggests a quality score. It never publishes on its own — it proposes changes as drafts for a human to approve. You are the human. Understanding what it does, and where your judgement overrides it, is covered in **AI Assist Features**.
