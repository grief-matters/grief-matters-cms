# Tagging & Classification

> **Important**
>
> If you read only one of these guides closely, make it this one.

**Classification is the editorial heart of the project.** The taxonomy documents you reference on a resource are what decide where it appears on the site and who it reaches. A superb resource that's poorly tagged is invisible to the person who needs it. A resource tagged onto the wrong page can reach someone it might distress.

## How tagging actually reaches people

The live site's pages are, under the hood, built from our taxonomy documents. A page like "grief after suicide" is really _"every resource tagged with the **suicide** cause of death."_ When you apply a classification to a resource, you are placing that resource on those pages. When you leave a relevant tag off, you remove it from a page where a grieving person was looking for exactly that.

So tag from the reader's point of view: _who would be looking for this, and what would they be looking for it under?_

> **Important**
>
> In case you hadn't guessed - **_this is the most important task when editing a resource_** - if we get this step wrong the resource may never be seen by those who need it

## Why you reference classification documents instead of typing out tags

You don't classify by typing free text — you classify by **referencing** an existing classification document. That's deliberate: it keeps the vocabulary controlled, so "suicide" always means the same thing site-wide, and so renaming or refining a tag updates it everywhere at once. It also means we can add rich metadata to each classification.

Each axis below has its own set of documents you can browse and reference (they appear in the Studio's classification section if you ever need to read a classification documents own description).

## The classification axes

Each axis answers a different question about the resource. You won't use every axis on every resource — use the ones that genuinely apply.

### Loss Relationship — who or what was lost

Parent, spouse, child, sibling, friend, pet, and so on.

> **Guidance**
>
> Most resources speak to a single relationship. If you're adding more than one, stop and ask whether the resource is really that broad?

### Cause of Death — how the death happened

Cancer, suicide, sudden/traumatic, overdose, and so on.

> **Guidance**
>
> Again, most resources address one cause.

### Grief Type — the kind of grief

Anticipatory, complicated, disenfranchised, and so on.

### Clinical Grief Phase — where in the grief arc it speaks to

e.g. acute destabilisation, meaning reconstruction. A resource may legitimately span several phases.

### Emotional State — the feelings it addresses

Anger, guilt, numbness, loneliness, and so on.

### Theme — the subject matter

Self-care, funerals & memorials, continuing bonds, and so on. A resource can span several _unrelated_ themes.

> **Important**
>
> You cannot tag both a theme **and** one of its sub-themes. The Studio will block this and tell you which pair conflicts. Always choose the **most specific** theme that applies — the resource still appears under the parent theme's page automatically.

### Demographic — a specific community it's made for

Use only when a resource is genuinely targeted at a particular community, not whenever it merely _could_ be relevant to one.

### Content Function — what the reader is trying to do

Practical help, validation, skill-building, and so on — the reader's _goal_ when the resource serves them well.

> **Guidance**
>
> Most resources serve 1–3 functions well. If you're adding many, the resource is probably being asked to do too much.

### Audience Role — whose lens it's written for (required)

- **Bereaved** — for the grieving person seeking help for themselves.
- **Supporter** — for someone helping another person who's grieving.
- **Professional** — for someone working with bereaved clients.

> This is **required**, and usually a single value. It's one of the most consequential tags: a resource written _for supporters_ should not surface as if it were written _for the bereaved_.

### Supported Griever — who's being supported

Appears **only** when Audience Role is _Supporter_ or _Professional_. It records attributes of the bereaved person being supported (e.g. Child, Teen) — so a supporter looking for help with a grieving teenager finds the right material.

## Principles for tagging well

- **Tag what the resource _is about_, not what it merely mentions.** A passing reference to guilt doesn't make it an "guilt" resource.
- **Prefer the most specific accurate tag.** Specificity is what makes the site useful; over-broad tagging makes every page a soup.
- **Fewer, truer tags beat more, looser ones.** The warning limits exist because over-tagging dilutes every page a resource lands on.
- **Tag for the reader's search, not the author's intent.** What would someone type or click to look for this?

## When you hit a boundary question — stop and ask

You _will_ run into genuinely hard calls:

> Is this complicated grief or disenfranchised grief? Is "continuing bonds" the right theme for this culturally specific practice?

Do not be afraid to reach out to the project lead for guidance on genuinely tough calls.

**The scope and meaning of the taxonomy is an editorial decision owned by the project's editorial lead.** Where one tag ends and another begins encodes editorial intent that hasn't always been fully written down. When you find a defensible-but-debatable boundary case, the right move is to **flag it for editorial review** rather than guess and move on.
